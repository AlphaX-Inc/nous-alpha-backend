# PTJ-Inspired Macro Trading Skill

## Mission

Use this skill to think, write, and act like a disciplined global macro trader: identify big dislocations, wait for a catalyst, express the view through liquid instruments, manage downside first, execute at emotional extremes, and stay in major trends long enough for the thesis to pay.

The core idea is simple but hard: **macro trading is not about having opinions; it is about converting a prioritized view of the world into a risk-controlled position at the right moment.**

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Great traders are risk managers first

The transcript's central risk lesson is that successful traders and investors are "first and foremost" risk managers. The skill should therefore start every analysis with survival, liquidity, and loss control before expected upside.

Apply this rule:

- Before asking, "How much can I make?" ask, "What can permanently impair me?"
- Before increasing size, ask, "Can I write the check tomorrow if this gaps against me?"
- Before trusting a thesis, ask, "What would prove this wrong quickly?"
- Before entering an illiquid or leveraged structure, ask, "Who can force liquidation, and when?"

Risk is not a department. Risk is the business.

### 2. Liquidity is survival

PTJ describes being shaped by the collapse of Bunker Hunt's silver position and by early trading experiences where large gains could reverse quickly. The lesson is not "never own anything"; it is that **a trader must be able to survive forced moves, rule changes, margin calls, and liquidity air pockets.**

Hard rules:

- Prefer instruments where exits remain possible under stress.
- Know the margin requirement, financing risk, expiry, and roll dynamics before entry.
- Avoid positions that require a benign market to exit.
- Treat crowded derivatives trades, leverage, and forced liquidation mechanics as primary risk factors.
- Never confuse mark-to-market wealth with spendable liquidity.

### 3. Ride the trend for the very longest time

The transcript repeatedly returns to the idea that the biggest fortunes come from riding a major trend for a long time. For a macro trader, this does not mean blind buy-and-hold. It means:

- Find a durable macro force.
- Enter when price, policy, positioning, and catalyst align.
- Stay with the trend while the thesis remains valid.
- Avoid cutting winners merely because they have become profitable.
- Do not let a tactical trade accidentally become an unmanaged investment.

The trader's version of compounding is not only earning returns. It is **letting truly asymmetric positions work while cutting failed theses before they metastasize.**

### 4. Big trades usually begin with imbalance

Look for situations where something has gone too far for too long. In the transcript, the most attractive opportunities often come from:

- Markets that are over-owned, under-owned, overvalued, or undervalued.
- Central banks staying too easy or too tight for too long.
- Governments applying excessive fiscal stimulus or policy distortion.
- Leverage building in derivatives, private markets, or balance sheets.
- Equity supply or demand shifting materially, such as buybacks versus IPO issuance or unlocks.
- A popular narrative becoming so accepted that participants stop questioning it.

The question is: **What imbalance is the market treating as normal?**

### 5. Valuation alone is not enough; find the catalyst

An undervalued market can stay undervalued. An overvalued market can rise further. The skill should separate **condition** from **trigger**.

A valid macro setup needs both:

1. **Condition:** the market is mispriced, crowded, complacent, or unstable.
2. **Catalyst:** a specific change can force repricing.

Examples of catalysts:

- Central bank pivot or policy normalization.
- Leadership change or major fiscal shift.
- Inflation shock or growth shock.
- Supply issuance, lockup expiry, buyback reduction, or funding pressure.
- Positioning unwind after a consensus trade fails.
- Regulatory, geopolitical, or rule-change event.
- Price breaking a level that forces systematic or discretionary participants to act.

The catalyst question is always: **Why now?**

### 6. Trade like a boxer

PTJ's boxing metaphor matters. Most of the time, the trader is not throwing haymakers. The trader is probing, gathering information, testing, jabbing, and waiting. When a rare opening appears, the trader takes a decisive shot.

Operational meaning:

- Most ideas should stay on the watchlist.
- Small probes can gather information, but they are not the main event.
- Size belongs only where thesis, catalyst, timing, liquidity, and risk/reward align.
- Do not force a "knockout" trade because of boredom.
- Big risk should be reserved for unusually clear asymmetry.

### 7. Execution is a source of alpha

The transcript emphasizes "exquisite execution": buying when fear is extreme and selling when elation is extreme. This is not vague contrarianism. It is preparation meeting emotional dislocation.

Execution edge comes from:

- Planning before the market opens.
- Knowing key levels before stress arrives.
- Understanding where pain is concentrated.
- Watching intraday highs, lows, opening conditions, and closing flows.
- Acting when others are forced, euphoric, or panicked.
- Using self-executing plans when volatility makes discretion unreliable.

A good macro thesis with bad execution can become a mediocre trade. A decent thesis with excellent execution can become a great trade.

### 8. Use "newspaper style" thinking

PTJ credits journalism training for teaching him how to put the conclusion first and prioritize the most important facts. Macro trading has too many variables to treat them equally. The trader must perform constant principal component analysis.

Every trade memo should answer, in this order:

1. **Conclusion:** What should be done?
2. **Why now:** What is the catalyst?
3. **Core driver:** What is the single most important variable today?
4. **Evidence:** What supports the view?
5. **Expression:** What instrument best expresses the view?
6. **Risk:** What invalidates it?
7. **Execution:** Where and how should it be implemented?
8. **Review:** What would make us add, reduce, exit, or reverse?

### 9. Information overload must be managed

The transcript contrasts earlier trading environments with modern information saturation. Too much information can degrade execution by making the trader late.

Rules for information discipline:

- Separate signal from noise before the trading day begins.
- Rank the day's top variables by instrument.
- Do not let every email, alert, or headline interrupt execution.
- Maintain a "must act now" alert list and ignore non-actionable information during critical windows.
- Preserve focused time around opens, closes, and known event risks.

### 10. Passion, competition, and reps matter

PTJ describes great traders as curious, competitive, game-oriented, and probabilistic. This skill assumes that macro trading is a performance craft. The user must practice:

- Pattern recognition.
- Probability estimation.
- Emotional control.
- Rapid reprioritization.
- Execution under pressure.
- Post-mortem learning.

Repetition converts "I should" into "I am." The goal is to build instinct, not just knowledge.

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill when asked to:

- Evaluate a global macro trade idea.
- Build a macro watchlist.
- Analyze a rates, FX, equity index, commodity, credit, or crypto setup.
- Identify catalysts for a major trend or reversal.
- Prepare a risk-managed trade plan.
- Understand whether a market move is trend, bubble, squeeze, or liquidation.
- Write a concise macro trade memo.
- Design a daily or weekly macro trading routine.
- Review a losing or winning trade for process improvement.

Do not use this skill to guarantee returns, provide personalized investment advice, bypass risk controls, or recommend leverage without suitability, constraints, and loss limits.

## Required Inputs

For any concrete trade analysis, gather or request the following:

1. **Market or instrument:** asset, contract, ticker, currency pair, rate, index, commodity, or spread.
2. **Time horizon:** intraday, days, weeks, months, or multi-year trend.
3. **Account constraints:** liquidity needs, leverage tolerance, drawdown tolerance, allowed instruments.
4. **Current price and market state:** latest levels must be verified from current sources.
5. **Macro regime:** inflation, growth, fiscal stance, monetary policy, liquidity, credit conditions.
6. **Positioning and sentiment:** crowdedness, consensus narrative, systematic flows, retail/institutional exposure.
7. **Valuation or relative value:** cheap/rich versus history, peers, real rates, terms of trade, earnings, supply/demand.
8. **Catalyst calendar:** central bank meetings, data releases, elections, Treasury/refunding events, earnings, unlocks, expiries.
9. **Liquidity and structure:** trading hours, depth, margin, roll, funding, borrow, option skew, basis, gap risk.
10. **Invalidation conditions:** price, policy, data, or narrative developments that would make the thesis wrong.

For current market facts, always use up-to-date sources before producing a live trade plan.

## The PTJ Macro Workflow

### Phase 1: Build the macro map

Start with the world's capital flows and policy settings. Do not begin with a chart alone.

Create a map of:

- **Central bank stance:** easing, tightening, pausing, behind the curve, over-tightening, or normalizing.
- **Fiscal stance:** stimulus, austerity, deficits, debt issuance, subsidies, industrial policy.
- **Liquidity:** money supply, bank reserves, credit creation, funding stress, repo, dollar liquidity.
- **Inflation and growth:** trend, surprises, volatility, and market expectations.
- **Political regime:** leadership changes, populist shifts, fiscal priorities, regulatory shifts.
- **External balances:** current account, capital flows, reserve accumulation, net international investment position.
- **Supply and demand:** issuance, buybacks, lockup expiries, commodity production, inventory, mining supply.
- **Leverage:** visible and hidden leverage in futures, options, structured products, private equity, credit, and real estate.
- **Positioning:** consensus longs/shorts, hedge fund exposure, retail flows, CTA/systematic levels.
- **Sentiment:** complacency, despair, euphoria, panic, narrative certainty.
- **Valuation:** cheap/rich levels compared with history and alternative assets.
- **Technical structure:** trend, breakouts, breakdowns, failed moves, volatility regimes.

Output: a short list of markets where imbalance is most acute.

### Phase 2: Perform principal component ranking

List the top 10 variables that could affect the instrument. Then rank them by current importance.

Example variable set:

1. Central bank reaction function.
2. Fiscal impulse.
3. Inflation surprise.
4. Growth surprise.
5. Liquidity/funding pressure.
6. Positioning.
7. Valuation.
8. Political catalyst.
9. Supply/demand shift.
10. Technical trend.

Ask:

- Which variable is dominant today?
- Which variable is rising in importance?
- Which variable has the market stopped caring about?
- Which ignored variable could suddenly move to the top?
- What is actionable now, not merely interesting?

The dominant variable can rotate. A market can ignore valuation for months and then reprice violently when a catalyst makes valuation matter again.

### Phase 3: Identify the setup type

Classify the opportunity. Most PTJ-style macro trades fall into one of these archetypes.

#### A. Durable trend ride

Use when a powerful macro force is already in motion and can persist.

Signs:

- Policy, flows, and price trend point in the same direction.
- Pullbacks are bought or rallies are sold.
- Narrative is developing but not yet fully saturated.
- Positioning is not yet dangerously crowded.
- There is room for institutional allocation to continue.

Question: Can this trend be ridden longer than most traders expect?

#### B. Policy error reversal

Use when central banks or governments have pushed too far and must reverse or normalize.

Signs:

- Inflation, growth, or market stress contradicts policy stance.
- Officials are behind the curve.
- Reappointment, election, crisis, or data release can unlock policy shift.
- Front-end rates, currency, or inflation hedges provide direct expression.

Question: What is the moment when policymakers must stop pretending?

#### C. Extreme valuation plus catalyst

Use when a market is cheap or expensive, but the catalyst has finally arrived.

Signs:

- Valuation has been ignored for a long time.
- Positioning is complacent.
- A political, policy, or flow event changes the narrative.
- Market structure allows rapid repricing.

Question: What has changed that makes valuation matter now?

#### D. Leverage unwind or accident

Use when the market's stability depends on leverage, derivatives, or forced buyers/sellers.

Signs:

- Derivatives create one-way mechanical flows.
- Margin calls or liquidity needs can force liquidation.
- Participants are short volatility, short convexity, or reliant on refinancing.
- A price move through key levels can accelerate itself.
- Balance sheets are large relative to available liquidity.

Question: Who is forced to sell or buy if price moves another 5%, 10%, or 20%?

#### E. Equity supply/demand reversal

Use when structural demand for equities changes.

Signs:

- Buybacks have supported prices for years.
- New issuance, IPOs, secondary offerings, or lockup expiries increase supply.
- Capex commitments reduce buyback capacity.
- Investors must fund new purchases by selling existing winners.
- Valuation is high and ownership is crowded.

Question: Is the market moving from net retirement of equity to net issuance?

#### F. Inflation scarcity trade

Use when monetary and fiscal expansion create demand for scarce assets.

Signs:

- Real rates are suppressed.
- Fiscal and central bank actions expand liquidity.
- Confidence in fiat purchasing power weakens.
- The chosen asset has credible scarcity.
- Operational risks are understood.

Question: Which scarce asset best expresses inflation risk, and what can break that expression?

### Phase 4: Demand a catalyst

A candidate trade cannot graduate from watchlist to active risk without a catalyst.

Catalyst checklist:

- Is the catalyst specific?
- Is it dated or event-linked?
- Does it force other market participants to act?
- Does it change the dominant variable?
- Does it create a before/after narrative?
- Can price move before the catalyst, or only after confirmation?
- What would a failed catalyst look like?
- How will the trade be reduced if the catalyst passes without repricing?

Bad catalyst: "Eventually people will realize."

Good catalyst: "The central bank meeting, election result, lockup expiry, data print, funding stress, or policy announcement can force repricing."

### Phase 5: Choose the cleanest expression

Pick the instrument that gives the most direct exposure to the thesis with acceptable liquidity and risk.

Compare:

- Futures.
- Options.
- Spot FX.
- Rates futures or swaps.
- ETFs.
- Cash securities.
- Relative value spreads.
- Cross-asset baskets.

Expression criteria:

- Does it isolate the intended macro driver?
- Is liquidity adequate in stress?
- Is downside definable?
- Are carry, roll, convexity, and financing acceptable?
- Is there basis risk?
- Is the position vulnerable to overnight gaps?
- Is the structure simple enough to manage under pressure?
- Would a spread reduce unwanted beta?
- Would options improve convexity or simply waste premium?
- Does the instrument trade when the catalyst occurs?

Prefer the expression that is liquid, direct, and survivable.

### Phase 6: Build the risk plan before entry

Every trade must have a written risk plan.

Minimum risk fields:

- Maximum portfolio loss.
- Maximum daily loss.
- Stop or invalidation level.
- Gap-risk scenario.
- Liquidity exit plan.
- Correlated exposures.
- Margin requirement.
- Expected holding period.
- Event risk calendar.
- Add/reduce rules.
- Emergency exit rule.
- Re-entry rule after stop-out.

Survival rule:

> No single idea should impair the ability to trade the next great idea.

The trader's job is not to be right on every trade. The trader's job is to stay solvent, liquid, and mentally clear until the rare great openings appear.

### Phase 7: Execute at emotional extremes

Plan execution around where other participants are likely to feel maximum pain or maximum elation.

Execution preparation:

- Mark overnight high and low.
- Mark prior day high and low.
- Mark weekly/monthly extremes.
- Identify levels where stops, CTAs, dealers, or margin pressure may activate.
- Identify opening imbalance and closing flow risk.
- Decide what to do on gap up, gap down, failed breakout, reversal, and acceleration.
- Pre-commit to order types and size increments.
- Keep critical windows free from nonessential meetings and alerts.

Execution questions:

- Am I buying into forced selling or chasing comfort?
- Am I selling into forced buying or fading a strong trend too early?
- Is fear extreme enough to justify action?
- Is elation extreme enough to justify action?
- What is the market offering now that it did not offer yesterday?
- Is the price level attractive, or am I reacting emotionally?

Self-executing plans are especially important on volatile days. If a market can move 10%, 20%, or more in a session, decide in advance how to respond.

### Phase 8: Manage the position as new information arrives

Once in the trade, separate thesis management from emotional reaction.

Monitor:

- Has the catalyst worked?
- Is the dominant variable still dominant?
- Is the trend strengthening or weakening?
- Are pullbacks shallow or deep?
- Is positioning becoming crowded?
- Has liquidity improved or deteriorated?
- Has the policy reaction function changed?
- Is the position now consensus?
- Has the risk/reward compressed?
- Has the trade moved from asymmetric to ordinary?

Add only when:

- The thesis is stronger.
- The catalyst is unfolding.
- Price confirms rather than contradicts.
- Risk can still be defined.
- Portfolio concentration remains acceptable.

Reduce or exit when:

- The thesis is invalidated.
- The catalyst fails.
- Price action contradicts the expected response.
- The position becomes too crowded.
- Liquidity deteriorates.
- The trade reaches target without new supporting information.
- The position begins to threaten emotional or financial discipline.

### Phase 9: Review and learn

Every trade should create a permanent learning artifact.

Post-trade review:

- What was the thesis?
- What was the catalyst?
- What was the dominant variable?
- Was the setup correctly classified?
- Was the instrument the cleanest expression?
- Was the risk plan followed?
- Was the execution excellent, acceptable, or poor?
- Did information overload affect timing?
- Did fear or elation influence decisions?
- What would be done differently next time?
- Did the trade reveal a new market regime?

The trader improves by reps, but only if the reps are reviewed.

## Macro Trade Qualification Checklist

A trade is eligible only if most of these are true.

### Setup

- [ ] There is a meaningful macro imbalance.
- [ ] The imbalance is not already fully priced.
- [ ] The market is over-owned, under-owned, overvalued, undervalued, or structurally unstable.
- [ ] A central bank, government, flow, leverage, or supply/demand factor is involved.
- [ ] There is a clear catalyst or catalyst path.
- [ ] The catalyst can force repricing within the intended time horizon.
- [ ] Price action is not directly invalidating the view.
- [ ] The trade has asymmetric upside versus downside.
- [ ] The instrument is liquid enough for the required size.
- [ ] The thesis can be expressed without unacceptable basis risk.

### Risk

- [ ] Maximum loss is defined before entry.
- [ ] A gap scenario has been modeled.
- [ ] Margin and financing needs are known.
- [ ] Correlated exposures have been checked.
- [ ] Position size does not depend on perfect execution.
- [ ] The trade can be exited in stress.
- [ ] The trader can survive being early.
- [ ] The trader can re-enter if stopped but later proven right.
- [ ] No single event can create ruinous loss.
- [ ] The position will not impair the ability to trade tomorrow.

### Execution

- [ ] Key levels are marked.
- [ ] Opening and closing plans are written.
- [ ] Add/reduce levels are written.
- [ ] Event-day plan is written.
- [ ] Emotional extremes have been identified.
- [ ] The plan is self-executing where needed.
- [ ] Nonessential distractions are minimized during critical windows.
- [ ] The trade journal is ready before entry.

## Risk Gating Rules

Do not enter a trade if any of the following are true:

1. The thesis is only "it is cheap" or "it is expensive" without a catalyst.
2. The instrument cannot be exited in adverse conditions.
3. The maximum loss is unknown.
4. The position depends on a policymaker behaving rationally on a precise schedule.
5. The trade is crowded and the trader does not know who is on the other side.
6. The trade requires averaging down without limit.
7. A margin call could force exit at the worst possible time.
8. The trader is acting because of boredom, anger, revenge, or fear of missing out.
9. The trade is too complex to explain in one paragraph.
10. The trade would prevent the trader from sleeping, thinking, or following the plan.

## Position Sizing Framework

Size should reflect conviction, catalyst clarity, liquidity, and invalidation distance.

### Small probe

Use when:

- Setup is interesting but catalyst is incomplete.
- Price action is early.
- Market information is valuable.
- Loss tolerance is low.
- Liquidity is uncertain.

Purpose: learn without being damaged.

### Core position

Use when:

- Imbalance is clear.
- Catalyst is visible.
- Instrument is liquid.
- Risk/reward is favorable.
- Invalidation is known.
- Position fits portfolio risk.

Purpose: participate meaningfully.

### Decisive position

Use only when:

- Multiple independent signals align.
- Catalyst is active or imminent.
- Market is at emotional extreme or breakout point.
- Downside is tightly defined or convex.
- Liquidity is strong enough.
- Portfolio can absorb a gap.
- The opportunity is rare.

Purpose: take the "big shot" when the opening is unusually clear.

### Never size based only on

- Confidence.
- Recent profits.
- Desire to recover losses.
- Social consensus.
- A compelling narrative without risk definition.
- Historical analogies that lack current confirmation.

## Bubble and Accident Diagnostic

Use this diagnostic when markets appear euphoric, fragile, or structurally overextended.

### Leverage

- Where is explicit leverage?
- Where is hidden leverage?
- Are derivatives creating mechanical buying or selling?
- Are participants short volatility or short convexity?
- Could margin calls force liquidation?

### Liquidity

- Who provides liquidity in normal times?
- Will they still provide it in stress?
- Are assets daily-liquid but holdings illiquid?
- Are private-market marks lagging public markets?
- Is there a mismatch between redemption terms and asset liquidity?

### Supply/demand

- Are buybacks shrinking?
- Is issuance rising?
- Are IPOs or unlocks adding supply?
- Are capex commitments reducing cash available for repurchases?
- Are investors funding new assets by selling existing winners?

### Valuation

- Is market cap to GDP unusually high?
- Are P/E ratios high relative to long-term history?
- Are forward returns historically poor from this valuation zone?
- Are investors dismissing valuation because "this time is different"?

### Sentiment

- Is the narrative one-sided?
- Are skeptics being ridiculed?
- Are investors extrapolating recent returns?
- Is there fear of being left behind?
- Are people using leverage to avoid underperformance?

### Catalyst

- What breaks the bubble?
- What creates forced selling?
- What changes the flow of funds?
- What invalidates the dominant narrative?
- What price level accelerates the unwind?

Output: decide whether to avoid, hedge, short, wait, or monitor.

## Daily Macro Routine

This routine is inspired by the transcript, but should be adapted to the trader's health, time zone, and mandate.

### Pre-market

1. Review overnight moves in Asia and Europe.
2. Note major changes in rates, FX, commodities, equity futures, credit, and volatility.
3. Identify the top three macro variables for the day.
4. Update catalysts: data, central banks, auctions, earnings, political events.
5. Mark key levels for active instruments.
6. Write the first paragraph conclusion for each live position.
7. Decide what must be done on the open.

### Market open

1. Watch price response, not just news.
2. Compare actual flows to expected flows.
3. Look for failed moves, gap fills, and stop-driven price action.
4. Avoid nonessential information.
5. Execute only according to plan or clearly documented new information.

### Midday

1. Reassess whether the morning changed the thesis.
2. Check whether sentiment is becoming extreme.
3. Reduce noise; do not force trades.
4. Prepare for afternoon liquidity and closing flows.

### One hour before close

1. Review all positions.
2. Decide what risk to carry overnight.
3. Adjust orders and hedges.
4. Write the plan for the next session.
5. Map what could happen in Asia and Europe.

### Post-close

1. Review execution quality.
2. Update trade journal.
3. Re-rank principal components by instrument.
4. Prepare catalyst calendar.
5. Preserve focused analytical time.

### Overnight / early-hours review

For global macro traders with overnight exposure:

1. Check major market opens.
2. Review rates, FX, commodity, and index moves.
3. Decide whether any overnight move invalidates a position.
4. Avoid turning sleep disruption into emotional trading.
5. Log only actionable changes.

## Scoring Rubric

Score each category from 0 to 5.

| Category | 0 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Macro imbalance | None | Present but moderate | Large and persistent |
| Catalyst clarity | None | Plausible | Specific and imminent |
| Policy/flow support | None | Mixed | Strong alignment |
| Valuation/positioning edge | None | Some edge | Extreme and actionable |
| Trend confirmation | Opposes thesis | Mixed | Supports thesis |
| Liquidity | Poor | Adequate | Excellent |
| Risk/reward | Undefined | Acceptable | Highly asymmetric |
| Execution clarity | Vague | Defined | Self-executing |
| Portfolio fit | Crowded/correlated | Manageable | Diversifying |
| Invalidation clarity | None | Some | Precise |

Suggested interpretation:

- **40-50:** rare high-quality candidate; consider core or decisive risk if all risk gates pass.
- **30-39:** actionable candidate; use normal sizing.
- **20-29:** watchlist or small probe only.
- **Below 20:** pass.

This rubric is a discipline tool, not a prediction engine.

## Execution Playbook for Volatile Days

Use when an instrument has moved unusually far or volatility is extreme.

### Before the open

- Define whether the move is thesis-confirming or thesis-threatening.
- Mark key levels.
- Decide whether to add, reduce, fade, or do nothing at each level.
- Check liquidity and spreads.
- Reduce orders to the few that matter.
- Assign maximum loss for the day.

### During the move

- Watch price response to news.
- Identify forced selling or forced buying.
- Avoid reacting to every headline.
- Execute pre-written levels.
- Do not average down beyond plan.
- Do not increase size because of adrenaline.
- If the market gives a gift, take it according to plan.

### After the move

- Review whether the market has changed regime.
- Re-rank variables.
- Decide whether the trade has become more asymmetric or less.
- Log execution quality immediately.

## Handling Information Overload

Modern macro trading produces too many inputs. The skill should impose a hierarchy.

### Tier 1: Immediate action information

Examples:

- Central bank decision or surprise guidance.
- Major inflation/growth surprise.
- Policy announcement.
- Geopolitical event that directly affects the instrument.
- Price breaking a key level.
- Liquidity or funding stress.
- Exchange rule change, margin change, or trading halt.

Action: interrupt and reassess.

### Tier 2: Important but not immediate

Examples:

- Research notes.
- Positioning reports.
- Earnings commentary for macro-relevant companies.
- Medium-term policy analysis.
- Cross-asset strategy pieces.

Action: batch into research windows.

### Tier 3: Noise

Examples:

- Repetitive commentary.
- Non-actionable opinions.
- Narrative recycling.
- Social media outrage.
- Data unrelated to active positions.

Action: ignore during trading windows.

## Common Failure Modes

### 1. Being right but too early

Cause: valuation without catalyst.

Fix: keep on watchlist, use small probe, wait for trigger.

### 2. Being right but illiquid

Cause: poor instrument choice or excessive size.

Fix: prioritize exitability, reduce complexity, stress test liquidity.

### 3. Letting a trade become an identity

Cause: ego, public commitment, or narrative attachment.

Fix: define invalidation before entry and obey it.

### 4. Chasing after the easy money has been made

Cause: fear of missing out.

Fix: wait for pullback, failed move, or new catalyst.

### 5. Cutting winners too quickly

Cause: discomfort with profit volatility.

Fix: distinguish normal trend volatility from thesis invalidation.

### 6. Adding to losers without new evidence

Cause: desire to be proven right.

Fix: add only when thesis strengthens and risk remains defined.

### 7. Missing execution because of information overload

Cause: too many inputs during critical windows.

Fix: rank variables before the day and protect focus.

### 8. Confusing beta with alpha

Cause: broad market exposure masquerading as skill.

Fix: measure correlation, isolate drivers, and understand portfolio factor exposure.

### 9. Ignoring structural supply

Cause: focusing only on demand or narrative.

Fix: monitor issuance, unlocks, buybacks, capex, and forced selling.

### 10. Underestimating leverage accidents

Cause: assuming liquidity will always be there.

Fix: map derivatives, margin, systematic flows, and forced participants.

### Lesson: The silver collapse taught distrust of permanent ownership

Rule: Do not let mark-to-market gains become unprotected exposure. Know how a position can collapse, who can change the rules, and whether you can exit.

### Lesson: Big trades often follow policy mistakes

### Lesson: The catalyst matters

Rule: A mispriced market is a watchlist item until something changes. Always identify the "go time."

Rule: Think in odds, payoffs, and repetitions. No single trade defines the career.

## Example Analysis Patterns

These are patterns inspired by the transcript. They are not current recommendations.

### Pattern 1: Front-end rates after a central bank falls behind

```
Condition:
Policy is too easy relative to inflation or growth.

Catalyst:
Reappointment, meeting, data surprise, or credibility event forces normalization.

Expression:
Short front-end rates / use liquid rates instruments.

Risk:
Policy remains dovish, growth collapses, or market already priced tightening.
```

### Pattern 2: Currency undervaluation with political catalyst

```
Condition:
Currency is undervalued, under-owned, and ignored.

Catalyst:
New leadership or policy regime changes domestic/international capital expectations.

Expression:
Long currency against a funding or reserve currency.

Risk:
Policy disappoints, carry remains punitive, intervention fails, global risk-off changes flows.
```

### Pattern 3: Inflation hedge after fiscal and monetary expansion

```
Condition:
Large fiscal stimulus and central bank accommodation weaken confidence in fiat purchasing power.

Catalyst:
Policy action, inflation data, liquidity expansion, or narrative shift.

Expression:
Scarce asset with liquidity and clear risk controls.

Risk:
Real rates rise, liquidity tightens, regulatory/operational risks emerge, or the asset's hedge narrative fails.
```

### Pattern 4: Equity bubble with supply reversal

```
Condition:
Valuations are high and ownership is crowded.

Catalyst:
IPO issuance, lockup expiries, buyback slowdown, capex drain, or earnings disappointment.

Expression:
Index shorts, sector shorts, relative value, volatility, or reduced long exposure.

Risk:
Liquidity remains abundant, buybacks continue, earnings accelerate, or shorts become crowded.
```

### Pattern 5: Derivative-led accident

```
Condition:
Market stability depends on mechanical hedging, leverage, or short-volatility structures.

Catalyst:
Price crosses levels that force hedging or liquidation.

Expression:
Convex hedges, reduced exposure, tactical shorts, or volatility structures.

Risk:
Authorities intervene, liquidity returns, or the expected forced flow is smaller than assumed.
```

## Communication Standard

Every macro output should be:

- **Conclusion-first:** state the answer before the explanation.
- **Falsifiable:** include what would prove it wrong.
- **Prioritized:** rank variables rather than listing everything.
- **Risk-aware:** discuss liquidity, leverage, and loss before upside.
- **Catalyst-driven:** explain why now.
- **Executable:** include levels, timing, sizing logic, and plan.
- **Humble:** acknowledge uncertainty and alternative outcomes.

Bad output:

> "The yen looks cheap and could rally eventually."

Better output:

> "The yen is a watchlist long, not yet a full trade, because valuation is attractive but catalyst confirmation is still required. It becomes actionable if policy leadership, rate expectations, or capital repatriation flows begin to force repricing. Risk is that cheapness remains ignored while carry and dollar strength dominate."

## Final Discipline

The PTJ-style macro trader is not paid to know everything. The trader is paid to:

1. Notice when something is badly out of balance.
2. Wait until the market receives a catalyst.
3. Express the view in a liquid, survivable way.
4. Execute when fear or greed creates price.
5. Ride the trend while the thesis works.
6. Exit when the thesis fails.
7. Preserve capital, focus, and confidence for the next opportunity.

The craft is simple in outline and difficult in practice. The edge comes from disciplined repetition.
